\begin{letter}{The Editors \\ \emph{Information Fusion}}

\opening{Dear Editors,}

Please find enclosed our manuscript, \textbf{``Vote First, Judge on Ties: Cost-Aware Answer Selection for Language-Model Systems,''} submitted as a research article (29 pages including references; 47 references). An online supplement with proofs and extended tables is provided as supplementary material.

\textbf{Aim and fit with the journal.} The paper treats the selection of one answer from several sampled language-model answers as a decision-level fusion problem. The candidates are noisy sources, a vote fuses them at no cost, and a pairwise judge is a costly second source that can resolve disagreement. The journal lists decision fusion, multi-classifier and decision systems, fusion system design, and the optimization of computational resources among its topics, and welcomes fundamental theoretical analyses. The paper contributes such an analysis for this fusion setting and tests it on public benchmarks.

\textbf{Main findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item Voting first and consulting the judge only on tied answers is Bayes-optimal in a finite-pool model. It is never worse than voting or than judging every problem, so it needs no judge-accuracy threshold.
\item When wrong answers collide, the Bayes-optimal rule for consulting a judge is a posterior-odds gate: the vote margin carries prior log-odds, each judge comparison adds a fixed log-odds step, and the judge should overrule a vote only when it outweighs it, with a closed-form margin threshold; the sequential version with a per-comparison cost is solved exactly. The exact accuracy and judge cost of margin-gated judging and the judge accuracy each collision level requires are computed. At the collision rates measured on stored runs the required accuracy (0.84 to 0.99) is far above that of the tested judge (0.55 to 0.59), which predicts the null result.
\item Judging every problem pays only above an explicit break-even accuracy; for problems of differing difficulty it is set by an effective-odds ratio of difficulty moments, not by pooled accuracy.
\item On six public benchmarks (532 problems) with frozen candidate pools and paired exact tests, vote-first selection uses 4\% to 7\% of the judge comparisons that judging everywhere needs, removes the loss that judging everywhere causes in heterogeneous pools, and adds no detectable accuracy over voting because exact ties are rare. Universal self-consistency and two paid small judges do not beat voting either.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Answers to the journal's required questions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{Comparison with the state of the art.} Yes, with stated limits. Five key references: self-consistency voting (Wang et al., 2023), universal self-consistency (Chen et al., 2023), the aggregation-model comparison of Campagner et al. (2023) in this journal, and the analyses of voting versus reward-based selection of Di et al. (2025) and Komiyama et al. (2025). The audit compares our selectors against self-consistency voting and against universal self-consistency, run on all 538 stored candidate pools with the same judge model (one call per problem): it ties voting in homogeneous pools (89.5\% against 89.5\%) and is worse in heterogeneous pools (85.3\% against 92.6\%, 0 against 15 discordant problems, exact $p<0.001$). Further baselines are an exact Kemeny--Young ranking reference and offline selectors (minimum Bayes risk, confidence-weighted voting, a knockout tournament, Bradley--Terry). To test whether a better judge changes this, pairwise judging and universal self-consistency were repeated with two paid small models (Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT Luna; spend of about two dollars, partial coverage reported in the paper): neither beats voting, and the judges' accuracy on mixed pairs (0.64 to 0.71) stays far below the 0.84 to 0.99 that the paper's collision model requires. Not evaluated: trained verifiers and LLM-Blender (Jiang et al., 2023), which need training or a trained ranker, and any frontier-scale judge. The paper states these limits.
\item \emph{Public datasets.} Common choices are GSM8K, MATH, AIME, MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond for answer quality, and RewardBench for judge calibration. We used all of them. GPQA outputs contain password-gated question text, so those stored runs are not in the public archive and GPQA is excluded from the vote-first audit.
\item \emph{Validation measures.} Standard measures are accuracy against ground truth and the share of available headroom recovered. We report both, with paired exact McNemar tests on discordant problems, the judge's accuracy on RewardBench, and the number of exact ties and ties that contain a correct candidate.
\item \emph{Main claim and significance.} A priced arbiter should be consulted only where the free fusion rule is uninformative, and judging beyond ties pays only above a break-even that depends on difficulty spread and on how often wrong answers repeat. This gives builders of language-model systems a cost-aware design rule and a measurable test for when to buy more judge calls, rather than an assertion of priority.
\item \emph{Evidence.} Exact enumeration verifies optimality and dominance of vote-first fusion for pool sizes 3 to 6, the break-even and collision calculations are checked against simulation, and the stored-run audit shows no gain from judging everywhere and a 93\% to 96\% reduction in judge comparisons for vote-first selection at voting-level accuracy. The judge picks the second response in 66\% to 75\% of universal self-consistency replies, a position bias that explains part of its weakness. All findings concern one free-tier judge, not stronger judges.
\item \emph{Closest related work.} Decision-level aggregation and ensembles (Campagner et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2020), majority voting for crowdsourced labels (Yu et al., 2026), cost-aware ensemble selection (Birman et al., 2022), and analyses of best-of-$N$, voting and universal self-consistency for language models (Chen et al., 2023; Di et al., 2025; Komiyama et al., 2025). We differ in analyzing a complete finite tournament of binary comparisons against plurality and in auditing it on frozen pools against ground truth.
\item \emph{Prior publication.} No part of this work has been published, including in conference proceedings. Code and stored runs are archived on Zenodo, which is not a publication of the paper.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Data and reproducibility.} Code, stored runs and tables are deposited in a public repository (persistent identifier given in the submission form), consistent with the journal's Option~C. The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, and the author declares no competing interests.
