\begin{letter}{The Editors \\ \emph{Information Fusion}}

\opening{Dear Editors,}

Please find enclosed our manuscript, \textbf{``Pairwise Judging versus Voting for Language Model Answer Selection: Finite-Pool Theory and an Empirical Audit,''} submitted as a research article to \emph{Information Fusion}.

\textbf{Aim and fit.} Sampling several answers from a language model and returning one is a decision-level fusion problem: the fusion rule is either plurality voting (self-consistency) or a pairwise judge. The paper derives exactly when a binary pairwise rule beats plurality for three candidates, enumerates break-even thresholds for larger finite pools, and audits a judge-based selector against ground truth. This falls under multi-classifier and decision systems and decision-level fusion.

\textbf{Answers to the journal's cover-letter questions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{State of the art.} Yes. The audit compares against self-consistency voting (Wang et al., 2023), the standard decision-level fusion baseline, and against additional stored-output selectors. Five key references: Wang et al. (2023, self-consistency); Chen et al. (2023, universal self-consistency); Jiang et al. (2023, LLM-Blender); Komiyama et al. (2025, best-of-infinity ensembling); Ai et al. (2025, beyond majority voting). Trained verifiers and universal self-consistency are not run as baselines; the manuscript states why they are not interchangeable with a training-free judge.
\item \emph{Public datasets.} Commonly used: GSM8K, MATH-500, AIME, GPQA Diamond, MMLU-Pro and RewardBench. All six answer-selection benchmarks (532 distinct problems) and RewardBench for judge validation are used.
\item \emph{Validation measures.} Accuracy against ground truth, paired exact tests on discordant problems, Wilson intervals, and judge accuracy on RewardBench. All are applied; a weighted-ranking agreement measure is reported separately and not treated as correctness.
\item \emph{Main claim and significance for this community.} The pairwise judge helps only above an explicit judge-accuracy threshold that depends on candidate accuracy and pool size (80.35\% mixed-pair accuracy at 80\% candidate accuracy for three candidates). This gives practitioners a testable condition for choosing a judge over voting as the fusion rule, instead of assuming it helps.
\item \emph{Evidence.} For the tested free-tier judge there is no detected homogeneous-pool advantage over self-consistency (10 versus 13 discordant problems of 532), and the heterogeneous-pool selector is worse (10 versus 32 of 402, $p<0.001$). The findings concern the tested judge and configuration, not stronger judges in general.
\item \emph{Closest related work.} Komiyama et al. (2025) and Di et al. (2025) on voting versus reward-based selection; Ai et al. (2025) on aggregation of heterogeneous correlated outputs; Landesberg (2026) on judge scores versus best-of-$N$ decisions. The manuscript states the difference: a finite complete pairwise tournament compared exactly with plurality, plus a ground-truth audit.
\item \emph{Prior publication.} No part of this work has been published, including as a conference paper. Code, public stored runs and tables are archived on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24112045).
\end{enumerate}

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. The author declares no competing interests.
